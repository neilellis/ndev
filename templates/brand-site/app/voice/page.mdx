# Voice

{{name}} sounds like an experienced adviser: calm, direct and specific. It explains rather than sells, and it shows its evidence.

## Principles

- **Plain and precise.** Ordinary words, short sentences, one idea per sentence.
- **Evidence-led.** Make claims you can support from the website or the published essays. Say what is told, shown or measured.
- **People first.** Technology earns its place by solving a real problem for people.
- **Confident, not loud.** No hype, superlatives or exclamation marks.

## Mechanics

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| British English | organisation, programme, optimise, licence (noun) |
| Sentence case for headings, buttons and labels | “Book a conversation”, not “Book A Conversation” |
| The name | {{name}}. Spoken “{{nameSpoken}}” |
| Product | {{product}} — one word, capital L |
| Numbers | Words for one to nine in prose; figures for 10 and above. In narration scripts, always words |
| Dates | 12 October 2026 |
| Lists | Middle dot `·` to separate short items in a line: “Data · AI · Transformation” |
| Quotes | Curly quotes “ ” and apostrophes ’ |

## Claims

- Client names: say “our team has worked with”. Do not imply a current contract or endorsement.
- {{product}} screenshots show Meridian Grid, a fictional demonstration organisation. Always say so.
- Do not invent statistics, testimonials or case-study results.

## Calls to action

Use one action per view, written as what the reader does: “Book a conversation”, “Read the series”, “Explore {{product}}”. Avoid “Click here”, “Learn more” and “Submit”.
